# 1. Executive Purpose

This document establishes the academic architecture of the Audio University. It defines how the institution is organised academically, how qualifications and programmes are designed, how learning outcomes are mapped and assessed, how academic standards are governed, and how quality is continuously assured.

It is not a list of courses and it is not a content-production manual. It is the institutional blueprint that must exist before programmes are populated with courses and before courses are populated with units and lessons.

The architecture must support a serious, academically defensible higher-education institution whose provision can be evaluated by competent national authorities, recognised quality-assurance agencies, professional bodies and receiving institutions in other countries.

The institution shall design for international comparability from the beginning. It shall not, however, claim that it is internationally recognised, accredited, degree-awarding, or authorised to issue recognised qualifications until the relevant legal authorisations and external quality-assurance decisions have actually been obtained.

## 1.1 The central design principle

**Academic legitimacy is earned through lawful authority, transparent standards, qualified academic governance, demonstrable student achievement, independent quality assurance and continuous improvement. It cannot be created by website wording, course volume, famous source material, certificates, or a sophisticated technology platform.**

## 1.2 What this document establishes

1. Institutional academic hierarchy and academic ownership.
2. Qualification and programme architecture.
3. Programme learning outcomes and curriculum mapping.
4. Credit, workload, levels and progression principles.
5. Course, unit and lesson placement rules.
6. Assessment strategy and academic standards.
7. Academic governance, approval and review.
8. Faculty and external academic expertise requirements.
9. Online/audio-first delivery quality requirements.
10. Student admission, progression, recognition of prior learning and academic records.
11. Internal quality assurance and external review readiness.
12. Accreditation and recognition readiness roadmap.
13. Data model and implementation requirements for the platform.
14. Templates, approval gates, KPIs and implementation tasks.

# 2. Recognition, Accreditation and Legal Status: A Non-Negotiable Distinction

The ambition is to build an institution whose qualifications can earn credible recognition across jurisdictions. That ambition requires a staged and legally grounded route.

There is no single worldwide authority that automatically recognises every university or every qualification. Recognition is determined through applicable national laws, competent recognition authorities, institutional status, programme characteristics, quality assurance, and the rules of the country or institution assessing the qualification.

UNESCO's Global Convention on the Recognition of Qualifications concerning Higher Education establishes principles for fair, transparent and non-discriminatory recognition. It does not itself accredit an individual institution or automatically recognise every award. Its framework relies on competent authorities, reliable information and quality-assured systems.

The World Higher Education Database (WHED), maintained by the International Association of Universities, is an important international information resource for recognised higher education institutions and national systems. Listing is not a substitute for legal authorisation or accreditation.

The European Standards and Guidelines (ESG) provide a widely used framework for internal and external quality assurance in the European Higher Education Area. They are a quality-assurance framework, not a universal institutional licence.

INQAAHE's International Standards and Guidelines (ISG) provide an international reference for quality assurance agencies and diverse higher education provision. CHEA recognition directories are relevant when evaluating US accreditation organisations. None of these bodies should be represented as having approved this institution unless a formal decision says so.

## 2.1 Institutional status categories

The institution must always describe its current legal and academic status accurately. The following statuses are distinct:

| Status | Meaning | What may be claimed |
|---|---|---|
| Project / educational platform | A digital learning initiative not yet legally authorised as a higher education institution | Educational platform or learning provider, as legally permitted |
| Registered education provider | Entity registered under applicable local law, without necessarily having degree-awarding authority | Exact registration status and permitted activities |
| Licensed higher education institution | Institution authorised by the competent authority to provide specified higher education | Only the authorised provision and awards |
| Accredited institution | Institution has received a current accreditation decision from a recognised/competent accreditor | Exact accreditor, scope, status and dates |
| Accredited programme | A specific programme has received programme-level accreditation | Only that programme and accreditation scope |
| Degree-awarding institution | Institution has legal authority to confer specified qualifications | Only the qualifications and levels authorised |
| Internationally listed institution | Institution appears in a relevant international directory | The exact listing, without implying universal recognition |

## 2.2 Mandatory legal workstream

Before advertising formal degrees, enrolling students into regulated degree programmes, or issuing awards that imply recognised higher education status, the founders must obtain qualified legal and regulatory advice and establish:

- The country of legal establishment and principal academic operations.
- The competent ministry, regulator, licensing authority and quality-assurance authority.
- Whether an online-first or distance-learning institution may be licensed under that jurisdiction.
- Whether the institution may operate across borders and recruit international students.
- Which entity may legally own and operate the institution.
- Which entity may award qualifications and under what authority.
- Whether local rules require physical facilities, local academic leadership, minimum faculty, library access, student support, examination controls or residency.
- Rules for advertising, consumer protection, tuition, refunds, data protection and student records.
- Rules for use of the words “university”, “college”, “degree”, “bachelor”, “master”, “accredited” and equivalent terms.
- Whether foreign partnerships, validation, franchising or joint awards are permitted and how they must be approved.

**Gate L0 — Legal feasibility:** No formal degree launch may pass this gate until the legal route, regulator, permitted award types and operating requirements are documented.

## 2.3 Prohibited claims until verified

The platform, marketing team, founders and academic staff must not claim:
- “Recognised worldwide” or “accepted in every country”.
- “UNESCO accredited”, “UNESCO recognised” or equivalent.
- “Accredited” without naming the competent accreditor, scope and current status.
- “Internationally accredited” based solely on membership, a partnership, a paid badge or a private certificate.
- “Bachelor's degree”, “Master's degree”, “university credit” or regulated award status unless authorised.
- Guaranteed employment, professional licensure, immigration eligibility or equivalency.

Any claim of recognition must be accompanied by a verifiable official source and a precise explanation of its scope.

# 3. Foundational Academic Principles

All academic decisions must comply with these principles.

## 3.1 Student achievement over content volume

The institution measures success by what students can reliably know, explain, apply, create, analyse and demonstrate—not by audio hours, number of lessons, number of courses or time spent with the player open.

## 3.2 Outcomes-based design

Every programme begins with its intended graduate capabilities. Courses, units, lessons, learning activities and assessments are designed backwards from those outcomes.

## 3.3 Constructive alignment

For every course and programme:
- Intended learning outcomes describe the expected achievement.
- Teaching and learning activities enable that achievement.
- Assessment tasks validly measure that achievement.
- Criteria and rubrics make the standard explicit.

An outcome that is not taught or practised is not adequately supported. An outcome that is never assessed cannot be claimed as demonstrated.

## 3.4 Academic depth and progression

A programme must show a coherent progression from foundational knowledge to advanced application, integration, independent judgement and, where appropriate, original inquiry or professional practice.

## 3.5 Academic independence

The institution may study, compare and learn from the curricula and open educational materials of leading universities. It must develop its own approved curriculum, teaching materials and assessment instruments, respect intellectual property and avoid implying endorsement by source institutions.

## 3.6 Evidence and transparency

Every programme must have:
- A published programme specification.
- Published admission and progression rules.
- Defined learning outcomes.
- A coherent course structure.
- Credit and workload definitions where applicable.
- Assessment regulations.
- Completion requirements.
- Award rules.
- A documented approval and review history.

## 3.7 Equity, accessibility and inclusion

The institution must design learning for diverse learners, devices, bandwidth conditions, languages, disabilities and educational backgrounds. Accessibility is a design requirement, not a late-stage visual enhancement.

## 3.8 Academic integrity

The institution must protect the validity of assessment and awards. It must define plagiarism, collusion, contract cheating, unauthorised assistance, identity fraud, fabricated evidence and prohibited AI use, with fair procedures and appeals.

## 3.9 Continuous improvement

Academic quality is a continuous institutional responsibility. Approval is not the end of quality assurance; it is the beginning of monitored delivery and periodic review.

# 4. Institutional Academic Hierarchy

The official academic hierarchy is:

```text
University
└── Colleges
    └── Departments
        └── Programmes
            └── Academic Levels
                └── Terms
                    └── Courses
                        └── Units
                            └── Lessons
```

This hierarchy is the official academic content tree. It must be implemented consistently in the academic model and administration interface.

However, the hierarchy alone is not a complete qualification architecture. Additional cross-cutting entities are required, including:
- Qualification / Award
- Programme Version
- Programme Learning Outcome
- Course Learning Outcome
- Curriculum Map
- Credit / Workload Model
- Course Offering
- Assessment Strategy
- Admission Rule
- Progression Rule
- Prerequisite / Co-requisite
- Elective Group
- Capstone / Dissertation / Practicum
- Academic Regulation
- External Review
- Approval Record
- Student Academic Record

These entities must not be forced into the visible tree if they are logically cross-cutting. They should be represented as linked academic records.

## 4.1 University

The University is the top-level academic authority and owns:
- Institutional mission and academic strategy.
- Academic regulations.
- Quality assurance policy.
- Qualification framework policy.
- College establishment and closure.
- Academic governance.
- Institutional research and academic integrity policies.
- Award approval and conferral authority, subject to law.

## 4.2 College

A College is an academic grouping of related disciplines and programmes. It must have:
- A defined academic rationale.
- A dean or accountable academic lead.
- A portfolio of related departments or programmes.
- A staffing and resource plan.
- A quality monitoring responsibility.
- A clear relationship to institutional strategy.

Do not create colleges merely to make the catalogue look large. A college must represent a sustainable academic grouping.

## 4.3 Department

A Department is responsible for academic coherence in a discipline or field. It oversees:
- Subject standards.
- Programme design and delivery.
- Academic staffing.
- Curriculum currency.
- Research and professional engagement where applicable.
- Course ownership and academic review.

A department must not be a decorative category. It must have clear academic accountability.

## 4.4 Programme

A Programme is a coherent, approved course of study leading to a defined qualification or formally described learning award.

Each programme must have:
- Unique programme code and title.
- Owning college and department.
- Award/qualification type and level.
- Programme rationale and demand evidence.
- Admission requirements.
- Programme learning outcomes (PLOs).
- Curriculum structure and duration.
- Credit/workload model.
- Course list and course status.
- Assessment strategy.
- Progression and completion rules.
- Staffing, learning resources and student support plan.
- Delivery mode and language(s).
- External reference points.
- Approval, review and version history.

A collection of unrelated courses is not a programme.

## 4.5 Academic Level

Academic levels express increasing complexity, autonomy, integration and responsibility. The institution may use internal labels such as Level 100, 200, 300 and 400 for undergraduate progression, but these labels must not be assumed equivalent to a national qualifications framework until formally mapped.

Recommended internal progression descriptors:

| Internal level | Typical academic character |
|---|---|
| 100 — Foundation | Core concepts, terminology, basic methods, guided application and foundational academic skills |
| 200 — Intermediate | Connected concepts, structured problem-solving, analysis, selection of methods and application in familiar contexts |
| 300 — Advanced | Complex analysis, integration across topics, evaluation, professional or disciplinary judgement and less structured tasks |
| 400 — Honours / Integrative | Independent work, synthesis, research-informed reasoning, complex projects, advanced practice and defensible conclusions |

These are design descriptors, not an automatic equivalence to a country's Level 4/5/6/7 or any named framework. The mapping must be approved separately.

## 4.6 Term

A Term is an administrative and academic period within a programme. It must specify:
- Start and end dates.
- Teaching and assessment periods.
- Registration and add/drop rules.
- Assessment deadlines.
- Resit / reassessment windows.
- Breaks and progression decision dates.

The institution may adopt semesters, trimesters or another structure only after considering regulator requirements, student workload, staffing and assessment capacity.

## 4.7 Course

A Course is an approved academic component within one or more programmes. It has:
- A stable identity and code.
- A versioned specification.
- Course learning outcomes (CLOs).
- Credit/workload value, where applicable.
- Level.
- Prerequisites and co-requisites.
- Units and lessons.
- Learning activities.
- Assessment strategy and grading rules.
- Required resources and references.
- Course owner and reviewer.
- Approval and review history.

A course may be shared across programmes only when the learning outcomes, level, workload and assessment standard are demonstrably equivalent. Similar titles are not sufficient evidence.

## 4.8 Unit

A Unit is a coherent thematic or conceptual block inside a course. It groups related learning outcomes and lessons and may contain a unit-level assessment or learning activity.

A unit is not automatically a credit-bearing academic component unless the approved programme regulations explicitly define it as such.

## 4.9 Lesson

A Lesson is the smallest planned teaching and learning component in the content architecture. It must belong to a unit and course and must support one or more approved learning outcomes.

The detailed production requirements for lessons are governed by `ACADEMIC_CONTENT_DEVELOPMENT_STANDARD.md`. This document does not replace that standard.

# 5. Qualification Architecture

The institution must distinguish between:
1. Learning content.
2. Non-credit learning.
3. Credit-bearing courses.
4. Credit-bearing programmes.
5. Formal qualifications.
6. Professional or regulated qualifications.

The fact that a learner completes an online course does not automatically make it a university credit or degree component.

## 5.1 Award types

The institution must not publish an award type until its legal authority and academic requirements are confirmed.

Possible categories for future planning include:
- Certificate of participation or completion (non-credit, clearly labelled).
- Verified course certificate.
- Credit-bearing certificate.
- Diploma.
- Higher diploma or associate-level qualification where legally recognised.
- Bachelor's degree.
- Honours bachelor's degree.
- Postgraduate certificate or diploma.
- Master's degree.
- Doctoral qualification.

These are categories for regulatory and academic planning, not a declaration that the institution currently has authority to issue them.

## 5.2 Qualification specification

Every formal qualification must have an approved specification containing:

| Field | Required definition |
|---|---|
| Qualification title | Exact official award title |
| Awarding body | Legally authorised awarding entity |
| Qualification level | Level mapped to the applicable national framework |
| Entry requirements | Academic, language and other criteria |
| Learning outcomes | Knowledge, skills, autonomy and responsibility |
| Credit volume | Defined using the authorised credit system |
| Nominal duration | Normal expected completion period |
| Maximum duration | Permitted completion window |
| Programme structure | Required, elective and optional components |
| Assessment | Methods and pass requirements |
| Progression | Conditions for continuation |
| Completion | All academic and administrative conditions |
| Classification | Award classification rules, if applicable |
| Exit awards | Approved interim awards, if any |
| Recognition of prior learning | Permitted process and limits |
| Delivery mode | Online, distance, blended or other approved mode |
| Language | Language of instruction and assessment |
| External reference | Relevant framework or professional standard |
| Approval record | Authority, date, conditions and review date |

## 5.3 Exit awards and stackability

The institution may consider progressive or stackable awards, but must not assume that several course certificates automatically combine into a diploma or degree.

An exit award is permitted only when:
- It is legally authorised.
- Its learning outcomes and credit volume are defined.
- Its assessment and quality assurance are approved.
- Its relationship to the parent qualification is transparent.
- Students are informed of any limits on progression or transfer.

# 6. Credit and Student Workload Framework

Credit is a measure of defined learning achievement and associated workload within a recognised academic system. It is not simply a count of audio minutes or video hours.

The institution must select its credit system only after the legal jurisdiction and target qualification framework have been identified. It must not mix credit systems or convert them by guesswork.

## 6.1 Workload components

Total student workload may include:
- Listening to audio lessons.
- Reading required and recommended material.
- Guided practice.
- Independent study.
- Problem sets and exercises.
- Labs, simulations and practical work.
- Discussion and collaborative activity.
- Research and project work.
- Assessment preparation.
- Assessment completion.
- Reflection and revision.

The audio duration is only one part of total workload.

## 6.2 Credit policy requirements

A formal credit policy must define:
- The selected credit system and its legal basis.
- The relationship between credit and notional learning hours.
- Whether credits are assigned at course or module level.
- Minimum and maximum term workload.
- Programme credit totals.
- Rules for transfer credit.
- Rules for recognition of prior learning.
- Rules for credit expiry, where relevant.
- Treatment of failed, repeated and withdrawn courses.
- Treatment of non-credit activities.
- Credit accumulation and award requirements.

## 6.3 No premature credit claims

Until the credit system is approved, internal planning may use estimated workload hours, but the platform and public catalogue must not describe these estimates as transferable university credits.

# 7. Programme Design Method

Every programme must follow a formal design sequence. Programme creation must never begin by collecting attractive course titles.

## 7.1 Programme design sequence

```text
Institutional Mission
        ↓
Graduate Profile
        ↓
Discipline / Industry / Societal Need
        ↓
External Reference Points
        ↓
Programme Educational Aims
        ↓
Programme Learning Outcomes (PLOs)
        ↓
Qualification Level & Credit Framework
        ↓
Curriculum Structure
        ↓
Course Learning Outcomes (CLOs)
        ↓
Unit Learning Outcomes
        ↓
Lesson Learning Objectives
        ↓
Learning Activities
        ↓
Assessment Evidence
        ↓
Programme Evaluation & Improvement
```

## 7.2 Stage 1 — Need and rationale

The programme proposal must document:
- The educational or professional need.
- Intended student population.
- Evidence of demand.
- Employment, professional or societal relevance.
- Comparable programmes and market analysis.
- Consultation with employers, professional bodies, academics and prospective learners.
- Whether the programme duplicates an existing institutional programme.
- Resource and financial sustainability.
- Regulatory feasibility.

Demand evidence may include labour-market data, employer consultation, public policy, academic literature, professional standards and student research. Marketing interest alone is not enough.

## 7.3 Stage 2 — Graduate profile

Describe what a successful graduate should be capable of doing, in language that can be assessed.

A graduate profile should cover relevant dimensions:
- Disciplinary knowledge.
- Technical or professional skills.
- Critical thinking and problem-solving.
- Communication.
- Digital and information literacy.
- Ethical and responsible practice.
- Collaboration.
- Independent learning.
- Research and evidence evaluation.
- Leadership or entrepreneurship where relevant.

Do not force every dimension into every programme. Select what is genuinely relevant and define the evidence.

## 7.4 Stage 3 — Programme Educational Aims (PEAs)

PEAs describe the broad achievements expected of graduates after a period of professional or personal development following graduation. They are broader and more long-term than course outcomes.

PEAs should be informed by stakeholder consultation and reviewed periodically.

## 7.5 Stage 4 — Programme Learning Outcomes (PLOs)

PLOs describe what students must demonstrate by the time they complete the programme.

Each PLO must:
- Use observable and assessable language.
- Be appropriate to the qualification level.
- Be achievable within the programme.
- Be mapped to courses and assessments.
- Have evidence of achievement.
- Avoid vague verbs such as “understand” or “know” unless translated into observable performance.

Use appropriate verbs such as explain, calculate, analyse, design, implement, evaluate, justify, investigate, communicate, construct, diagnose, interpret or defend.

A programme will typically have a manageable set of PLOs. The number must be determined by programme scope, not by a universal quota.

## 7.6 Stage 5 — External reference points

Each programme must identify relevant external reference points, such as:
- National qualifications framework.
- National subject benchmark statements.
- Professional or statutory regulatory body standards.
- International disciplinary curriculum guidelines.
- Relevant accreditation criteria.
- Research literature and current disciplinary practice.
- Employer and professional consultation.

Examples of discipline-specific reference points include ACM/IEEE-CS/AAAI curriculum guidance for computing. Such guidance is a reference, not an accreditation decision.

## 7.7 Stage 6 — Curriculum structure

The curriculum must specify:
- Required core courses.
- Elective courses and elective groups.
- General or transferable education, where appropriate.
- Practical, laboratory, studio or field components.
- Research methods, where relevant.
- Internship, practicum or professional experience, where appropriate.
- Capstone, dissertation or final project, where appropriate.
- Course sequencing.
- Prerequisites and co-requisites.
- Credit/workload distribution.
- Assessment milestones.
- Programme completion rules.

Every required course must have a clear reason for being in the programme.

## 7.8 Stage 7 — Curriculum mapping

Every PLO must be mapped to courses and assessments.

Recommended contribution levels:

| Code | Meaning |
|---|---|
| I — Introduced | Students first encounter and practise the outcome |
| D — Developed | Students build depth, fluency and independence |
| M — Mastered / Demonstrated | Students demonstrate the outcome at the expected exit level |

The programme must show a logical progression. A PLO should not appear only in a final course without prior preparation unless the programme rationale explicitly justifies it.

A separate assessment map must show where each PLO is directly assessed.

## 7.9 Stage 8 — Validation

Before approval, the programme proposal must be reviewed by:
- Subject-matter academics.
- An academic outside the design team.
- Relevant employers or professional stakeholders.
- Quality assurance staff.
- Student or learner representatives where feasible.
- Legal/regulatory advisers when award authority or professional regulation is implicated.

For high-stakes or regulated fields, independent external academic and professional review is mandatory.

# 8. Curriculum Coherence and Course Sequencing

A curriculum must form a coherent learning journey, not a collection of disconnected subjects.

## 8.1 Required curriculum checks

Each programme must pass the following checks:
- No required course is orphaned from programme outcomes.
- No PLO is unsupported by courses.
- No PLO is claimed without direct assessment evidence.
- Prerequisites are academically justified.
- Course workload is balanced across terms.
- Assessment deadlines are not excessively concentrated.
- Foundational knowledge is taught before it is assumed.
- Advanced courses build on prior learning.
- Practical skills are practised, not only described.
- Current developments are incorporated without destabilising core foundations.
- Students have appropriate opportunities for feedback and improvement.
- The programme culminates in appropriate integration of learning.

## 8.2 Course dependency graph

The platform should support prerequisite relationships as a directed graph.

Rules:
- Prerequisite cycles are prohibited unless an explicitly approved exceptional design exists.
- A prerequisite must be linked to a specific learning need.
- Co-requisites must be distinguishable from prerequisites.
- Waivers must be recorded with a reason and authorised decision.
- Course changes must trigger a review of dependent courses.
- Students must not be silently moved into a new curriculum version.

## 8.3 Core and elective balance

The programme specification must define:
- Required core courses.
- Elective groups.
- Minimum elective choice.
- Rules for substitutions.
- Limits on repeating equivalent content.
- How elective choices affect the student's transcript and award eligibility.

Electives must preserve the integrity of the qualification outcomes. A student must not be able to graduate without demonstrating all mandatory PLOs.

## 8.4 Capstone and integrative learning

Where appropriate to the discipline and qualification level, the programme should include a capstone, dissertation, portfolio, substantial project, practicum or equivalent integrative assessment.

It must:
- Require synthesis of prior learning.
- Have transparent assessment criteria.
- Be supervised or moderated appropriately.
- Include authenticity and authorship checks.
- Have a documented appeals and reassessment process.
- Be assessed against programme-level outcomes.

A capstone is not mandatory for every short course or every non-degree award.

# 9. Assessment Architecture and Academic Standards

Assessment is the evidence base for academic claims. The institution must not treat listening completion, clicks, time watched or quiz participation as sufficient proof of competence.

## 9.1 Assessment principles

Assessment must be:
- Valid: measures the intended outcome.
- Reliable: supports consistent judgement.
- Fair: accessible and non-discriminatory.
- Transparent: criteria and rules are published.
- Authentic: demonstrates meaningful student achievement.
- Secure: protects assessment integrity.
- Proportionate: appropriate to the stakes and level.
- Inclusive: reasonable accommodations are available.
- Timely: feedback supports learning and progression.

## 9.2 Assessment types

Depending on discipline and outcomes, use an appropriate mix:
- Diagnostic assessment.
- Formative quizzes.
- Problem-solving exercises.
- Written assignments.
- Oral explanation or viva.
- Coding tasks and practical labs.
- Case analysis.
- Research critique.
- Portfolio.
- Project or capstone.
- Practical demonstration.
- Timed examination.
- Peer and self-assessment where educationally justified.

No single assessment format is suitable for every discipline.

## 9.3 Assessment blueprint

Each course must have an assessment blueprint containing:

| Field | Required information |
|---|---|
| Assessment ID | Stable identifier |
| Assessment title | Clear name |
| Assessment type | Quiz, project, exam, practical, etc. |
| CLO mapping | Outcomes assessed |
| PLO contribution | Programme outcomes supported |
| Weight | Contribution to final grade |
| Timing | Due date or assessment window |
| Format | Individual, group, timed, open-book, etc. |
| Rubric | Criteria and performance levels |
| Pass rule | Minimum performance if applicable |
| Attempts | Number and conditions |
| Feedback | Type and expected timing |
| Moderation | Internal/external checks |
| Integrity controls | Identity, authorship and permitted assistance |
| Accessibility | Accommodations and alternatives |

## 9.4 Assessment coverage

The programme must provide direct evidence for all PLOs. A mapping table must identify:
- Where an outcome is introduced.
- Where it is developed.
- Where it is assessed at exit level.
- Which assessment instrument provides the evidence.
- Who reviews the evidence.

## 9.5 Grading policy

A formal grading policy must define:
- Grade scale.
- Pass threshold.
- Weighting and rounding rules.
- Late submission rules.
- Extensions and mitigating circumstances.
- Incomplete grades.
- Academic misconduct penalties.
- Resit and reassessment rules.
- Repeat-course rules.
- Grade appeals.
- Moderation and external review.
- Award classification, where applicable.

Do not select a grading scale by copying a foreign university. It must fit the applicable regulator and qualification framework.

## 9.6 Audio-first assessment safeguards

For audio-first courses:
- Audio completion is a learning activity record, not proof of mastery.
- Assessments must measure the outcomes rather than recall of incidental wording.
- Learners must be able to access transcripts or equivalent accessible formats where appropriate.
- Listening progress must not be manipulated into a grade unless the academic rationale and regulations explicitly support it.
- Practical, written, oral or project evidence must be included whenever the outcomes require performance beyond listening.
- The platform must distinguish content access, learning activity, assessment attempt and verified achievement.

# 10. Academic Level Descriptors

The University must adopt a formal set of level descriptors aligned to its chosen national qualifications framework before issuing formal awards.

The following generic descriptors are an internal design aid only.

| Dimension | Foundation | Intermediate | Advanced | Honours / Integrative |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Knowledge | Core terminology and principles | Connected concepts and methods | Critical and specialised knowledge | Systematic, current and integrated knowledge |
| Application | Guided tasks in familiar contexts | Structured tasks with method selection | Complex and less predictable tasks | Independent response to complex problems |
| Analysis | Identify and explain relationships | Analyse evidence and compare approaches | Evaluate assumptions and limitations | Synthesize evidence and defend conclusions |
| Autonomy | Works with guidance | Increasing responsibility | Substantial independence | Independent judgement and self-directed work |
| Communication | Clear basic explanation | Structured disciplinary communication | Audience-aware professional communication | Defensible, nuanced and research-informed communication |
| Inquiry | Locate and use supplied sources | Compare and interpret evidence | Critically evaluate sources and methods | Conduct or design independent inquiry where relevant |

These descriptors must be refined by discipline. A mathematics programme, nursing programme, computing programme and business programme cannot use identical evidence of competence.

# 11. Admission, Progression and Student Academic Lifecycle

Academic architecture must define the student's entire academic lifecycle, not only the course catalogue.

## 11.1 Admission policy

Every programme must publish:
- Entry qualifications.
- Subject prerequisites.
- Language requirements.
- Recognition of international qualifications.
- Application evidence.
- Selection criteria.
- Capacity limits where relevant.
- Accessibility and reasonable adjustment procedures.
- Appeal procedures.
- Conditional admission rules, if allowed.

Admission requirements must be proportionate to the programme and lawful in the relevant jurisdiction.

## 11.2 Diagnostic placement

Where appropriate, the institution may use diagnostic assessments to identify gaps and recommend foundation learning. Diagnostic results must not be confused with formal admission decisions unless approved regulations define them as such.

## 11.3 Progression rules

Programme regulations must define:
- Minimum course pass requirements.
- Prerequisite enforcement.
- Maximum failed or repeated courses.
- Academic probation and support.
- Suspension and withdrawal.
- Maximum completion period.
- Re-entry rules.
- Progression between levels.
- Exceptional-case decisions and appeals.

## 11.4 Recognition of Prior Learning (RPL)

The institution should develop an RPL policy only where permitted by law and the relevant qualification framework.

RPL must:
- Assess demonstrated learning, not merely years of experience.
- Map evidence to specific learning outcomes.
- Use trained assessors.
- Require authentic and current evidence.
- Apply consistent criteria.
- Record decisions and reasons.
- Provide appeal mechanisms.
- Protect the integrity of the qualification.
- Respect any limits on the proportion of a qualification that may be awarded through RPL.

Prior learning may include formal, non-formal and informal learning where the applicable rules permit it. Recognition is not automatic.

## 11.5 Transfer credit

Transfer credit requires:
- Verified prior study.
- Official transcript or reliable evidence.
- Comparison of level, outcomes, workload and assessment.
- Approval by an authorised academic decision-maker.
- Recorded rationale.
- Compliance with residency or minimum-study requirements where applicable.

Course-title similarity alone is not enough.

## 11.6 Student support and academic advising

The University must provide support appropriate to online and audio-first delivery:

# 14. Online and Audio-First Academic Delivery

The Audio University has an audio-first delivery identity. That is a delivery design choice, not a reason to lower academic standards.

The institution must demonstrate that its delivery mode can support the full range of intended outcomes for each programme.

## 14.1 Audio-first does not mean audio-only

Audio is highly suitable for explanation, conceptual development, guided reasoning, language learning and many forms of knowledge acquisition. It is not sufficient by itself for every outcome.

Where outcomes require:
- Coding: learners must write, run, debug and explain code.
- Laboratory science: learners need valid practical or simulation evidence.
- Clinical or professional practice: learners need supervised and appropriately verified practice where required.
- Design: learners must produce and critique artefacts.
- Mathematics: learners must solve problems and show reasoning.
- Communication: learners must demonstrate communication performance.
- Research: learners must formulate questions, evaluate evidence and produce research work.

The programme must use suitable practical, interactive, written, oral, simulation or supervised components.

## 14.2 Course delivery specification

Each course must define:
- Delivery mode.
- Required devices and software.
- Minimum connectivity assumptions.
- Offline or low-bandwidth options where feasible.
- Audio and transcript availability.
- Accessible alternatives.
- Interaction and feedback opportunities.
- Practical work requirements.
- Identity and assessment controls.
- Technical support arrangements.
- Data privacy and retention rules.

## 14.3 Student engagement and attendance

The institution must define what counts as participation for each course. Player events alone must not be represented as academic attendance or achievement unless the regulations establish a defensible method.

## 14.4 Accessibility

The University should adopt recognised accessibility standards for its digital platform and learning materials, including WCAG as an implementation reference. Accessibility requirements must cover:
- Keyboard navigation.
- Screen-reader compatibility.
- Captions and transcripts where applicable.
- Audio controls and playback speed.
- Colour contrast and readable typography.
- Clear navigation and consistent interaction.
- Alternatives to audio-only instructions.
- Accommodation processes for assessments.

The institution must test accessibility with real assistive technologies and users where possible.

# 15. Academic Quality Assurance Framework

Quality assurance must operate at four connected levels:

1. Institutional quality assurance.
2. College and department quality assurance.
3. Programme quality assurance.
4. Course, unit, lesson and assessment quality assurance.

## 15.1 Plan–Do–Check–Act

The institution should operate a documented continuous-improvement cycle:

```text
PLAN
Define outcomes, standards, resources and success measures
    ↓
DO
Deliver teaching, learning, assessment and support
    ↓
CHECK
Review data, student work, feedback and external evidence
    ↓
ACT
Approve corrective actions, enhancement and follow-up
    ↺
```

## 15.2 Programme approval

No programme may be published as an approved programme until it passes all required gates:

| Gate | Requirement | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| A1 | Legal feasibility | Regulatory and legal assessment |
| A2 | Strategic fit | Programme rationale |
| A3 | Demand and stakeholder need | Market and consultation evidence |
| A4 | Qualification level | Framework mapping |
| A5 | PLO quality | Approved outcomes and descriptors |
| A6 | Curriculum coherence | Curriculum map and sequencing |
| A7 | Assessment validity | Assessment blueprint and rubrics |
| A8 | Staffing and resources | Resource and staffing plan |
| A9 | Delivery suitability | Online/audio-first delivery review |
| A10 | Student support | Support and accessibility plan |
| A11 | Independent review | External academic review |
| A12 | Formal approval | Academic Council decision and record |

## 15.3 Course approval

A course must have:
- Approved course specification.
- CLOs mapped to PLOs.
- Course-level curriculum position.
- Workload and credit basis.
- Unit and lesson map.
- Assessment blueprint.
- Reading and source list.
- Accessibility and delivery plan.
- Academic reviewer sign-off.
- Version and approval record.

## 15.4 Annual programme monitoring

Every active programme must be reviewed annually. The report should include:
- Applications and admissions.
- Student demographics and access/equity indicators where lawful.
- Enrolment and retention.
- Course pass and failure rates.
- Withdrawals and reasons.
- Assessment performance.
- PLO achievement evidence.
- Student feedback.
- Complaints and appeals.
- Academic misconduct cases.
- External reviewer feedback.
- Staff changes and workload.
- Resource and technology issues.
- Actions from the previous year.
- New risks and improvement actions.

## 15.5 Periodic programme review

In addition to annual monitoring, every programme must undergo a deeper periodic review on a defined cycle, normally within a five-year planning horizon or sooner if required by the regulator or professional body.

The review should assess:
- Continued relevance and demand.
- Curriculum currency.
- Qualification-level appropriateness.
- PLO achievement.
- Assessment standards.
- Student progression and completion.
- Graduate outcomes where reliable data exist.
- Employer and professional feedback.
- External comparability.
- Staffing and resources.
- Equality, accessibility and student support.
- Whether the programme should continue, change, suspend or close.

## 15.6 External examination / moderation

Where appropriate and permitted, independent external academic review should examine assessment standards and student achievement. The institution must define:
- Reviewer eligibility and independence.
- Scope of review.
- Access to assessment briefs and anonymised student work.
- Confidentiality and data protection.
- Reporting format.
- Response and action tracking.

## 15.7 Student voice

Student feedback must be gathered through more than one mechanism:
- Course evaluations.
- Programme surveys.
- Student representation.
- Focus groups or interviews.
- Complaints and appeals.
- Exit feedback.

Feedback must be analysed, acted upon and closed-loop communicated. Survey scores alone are not proof of academic quality.

## 17.2 Required relationship rules

- A College belongs to the University.
- A Department belongs to a College.
- A Programme has one accountable owning Department; approved cross-departmental collaboration may be recorded separately.
- A Programme has immutable version history.
- A ProgrammeVersion contains a defined curriculum structure.
- A Course may be referenced by multiple programmes through ProgrammeCourse.
- ProgrammeCourse must store the role of the course (core, elective, option, prerequisite or other approved category).

- Units belong to a specific CourseVersion.
- Lessons belong to a specific Unit and are versioned.
- PLOs belong to a ProgrammeVersion.
- CLOs belong to a CourseVersion.
- Outcome mappings connect version-specific outcomes to version-specific courses and assessments.
- Assessments and rubrics are versioned.
- Student enrolments must record the programme and course versions applicable to the student.
- Academic records must preserve the exact versions and rules under which achievement occurred.
- Award decisions must be traceable to approved regulations and evidence.
- Approval and audit records must not be silently overwritten or deleted.

## 17.3 Versioning and student protection

A programme change must not retroactively alter a student's academic record.

Every student must be associated with:
- The programme version under which they enrolled.
- Any approved transition to a later version.
- Course versions completed.
- Assessment versions attempted.
- Rules used for progression and award decisions.

When a programme is changed or closed, the institution must approve a transition or teach-out plan defining:
- Which students are affected.
- Whether they may complete the old version.
- Equivalent replacement courses.
- Additional support.
- Maximum completion dates.
- Financial and student-protection arrangements.
- Communication and appeal rights.

## 17.4 Academic catalogue versus delivery instance

Separate:
- The canonical Course and CourseVersion.
- The CourseOffering delivered in a specific term.
- The enrolled student's participation and assessment records.

This allows one approved course to be delivered in multiple terms, languages or cohorts without corrupting the academic specification.

## 17.5 Academic policy as configuration

Where appropriate, configurable rules may include:
- Prerequisites.
- Progression thresholds.
- Assessment weighting.
- Attempt limits.
- Credit requirements.
- Course availability.
- Language availability.

However, high-impact academic rules must be approved, versioned, auditable and effective-dated. Administrators must not change them informally in production.

## 17.6 Security and audit

Academic records are sensitive and must follow the Blueprint's security requirements:
- Least-privilege access.
- Separation of duties.
- Strong authentication for privileged roles.
- Immutable audit trails for consequential academic decisions.
- Secure backups and tested restoration.
- Encryption in transit and at rest.
- Retention and deletion rules consistent with law.
- Student access to their own records.
- Controlled transcript and award verification.
- Incident response and breach notification procedures.

# 19. Academic Portfolio Strategy

The University must grow its academic portfolio deliberately.

## 19.1 Do not launch many colleges at once

A broad catalogue can create the appearance of scale while weakening quality, staffing and student trust. The institution should establish a narrow, defensible initial portfolio and expand only when it can demonstrate academic capacity.

## 19.2 Portfolio selection criteria

Each proposed programme should be scored against:
- Mission fit.
- Demonstrable learner and societal need.
- Academic expertise available.
- Regulatory feasibility.
- Availability of qualified faculty and reviewers.
- Access to suitable learning resources.
- Suitability for online/audio-first delivery.
- Assessment feasibility and integrity.
- Student support capacity.
- Financial sustainability.
- Potential for meaningful outcomes.
- Risk and reputational exposure.

A high market-demand score must not compensate for failure in legal feasibility or academic capacity.

## 19.3 Initial programme selection gate

The first programme should be selected only after:
1. Legal route is established.
2. Target learner and need are evidenced.
3. Programme outcomes can be assessed online.
4. Subject expertise and independent review are available.
5. Curriculum and assessment are complete.
6. Student support and accessibility are ready.
7. The programme can be delivered consistently.
8. The institution can monitor achievement and improve it.

The first programme is a proof of academic capability, not merely a marketing launch.

# 25. Academic Approval and Change Control

All changes to the academic architecture must be classified by impact.

## 25.1 Change categories

| Change | Minimum approval |
|---|---|
| Typographical or non-substantive description correction | Delegated academic owner |
| Lesson improvement without outcome/assessment impact | Course owner plus required content review |
| Change to CLO wording or course assessment | Academic course approval route |
| Change to course credit, level or prerequisites | Programme/academic approval authority |
| Change to required courses or curriculum structure | Programme committee and formal academic approval |
| Change to PLOs or qualification outcomes | Formal programme reapproval |
| Change to award title, level or credit volume | Formal institutional and regulatory approval as required |
| Programme suspension or closure | Formal approval and teach-out plan |
| Change affecting legal authorisation or accreditation scope | Regulator/accreditor approval where required |

## 25.2 Change impact analysis

Before approval, assess:
- Students currently enrolled.
- Programme and course versions.
- PLO coverage.
- Prerequisite dependencies.
- Credit and workload.
- Assessment validity.
- Staffing and resources.
- Regulatory and accreditation conditions.
- Public catalogue information.
- Transcript and award consequences.
- Required transition or teach-out arrangements.

No academic change may be deployed directly to production without an approved change record.

# 30. Final Institutional Rule

The University shall not confuse a well-designed learning platform with a recognised university, a completed course with a qualification, audio engagement with demonstrated competence, or international benchmarking with accreditation.

The architecture must make every academic claim traceable:

**Who authorised it? Which qualification framework defines it? Which outcomes does it promise? Where are those outcomes taught? How are they assessed? Who reviewed the standards? What evidence proves achievement? Which version and rules applied to the student?**

If the institution cannot answer these questions with documented evidence, the claim must not be made.

**The objective is not to look like a world-class university. The objective is to build the legal, academic, human and quality-assurance foundations that can withstand independent scrutiny by competent authorities and the international academic community.**
